\begin{abstract}
This document present an approach to optimize the Adaptive Data Rate (ADR) mechanism in LoRaWAN networks using reinforcement learning (RL) methods. In dense IoT deployment where many devices transmit frequently in close radius, packet losses happen due to collisions and interference. The classical ADR algorithm that LoRaWAN use cannot adapt well to these dynamic conditions. We model the problem as a Markov Decision Process and apply three RL algorithms (Double Deep Q-Network with Prioritized Experience Replay (DDQN-PER), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)) to dynamically adjust spreading factor (SF) and transmission power (TP) for each device. We build the simulation environment on top of ns-3 network simulator with realistic interference from WiFi and LTE mobile devices. Our results show that RL based ADR can achieve up to 99.65\% packet delivery rate compare to 40\% with classical ADR in dense scenarios, while MARL provide significant energy savings. We also implement a physical testbed using ESP32 devices with SX1278 LoRa modules and a STM32 based gateway to validate our approach in real world.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Background}
\label{subsec:background}

Long-range wide area networks (LoRaWANs) have emerged as a cornerstone technology in the Internet of Things (IoT) landscape, gaining widespread adoption due to their exceptional long-range communication capabilities, ultra-low power requirements, and cost-effectiveness \cite{Raza17}. As one of the leading Low Power Wide Area Network (LPWAN) protocols, LoRaWAN is particularly well-suited for large-scale IoT applications spanning diverse domains including smart cities, industrial monitoring, precision agriculture, logistics, environmental sensing, and smart campuses \cite{Maurya2025LoRaWANsurvey}. These applications typically require devices to operate autonomously over extended periods, often spanning several years on a single battery charge, while covering geographically distributed areas that can extend from hundreds of meters to several kilometers. The ability of LoRaWAN to connect thousands of low-power devices with minimal infrastructure investment makes it a critical enabler of digital transformation in both urban and rural contexts \cite{augustin2016study}.

LoRaWAN networks employ a characteristic star-of-stars topology architecture, wherein battery-powered end devices communicate with one or more gateways using the LoRa physical layer modulation scheme. These gateways, which are typically mains-powered and strategically positioned to maximize coverage, act as transparent bridges that forward packets to centralized network servers via standard IP-based backhaul connections such as Ethernet, WiFi, or cellular networks \cite{adelantado2017understanding}. The network server, residing in the cloud or on-premises, manages the entire network, processes application data, handles device management, and implements network-level optimization strategies.

The LoRaWAN protocol incorporates several sophisticated mechanisms designed to optimize network performance and device longevity. The Adaptive Data Rate (ADR) algorithm dynamically adjusts transmission parameters such as spreading factor and transmission power based on link quality feedback, enabling devices to minimize energy consumption while maintaining reliable communication \cite{cuomo2017explora}. The protocol also implements duty cycle limitations mandated by regional regulations in unlicensed Industrial, Scientific, and Medical (ISM) bands, typically restricting transmission time to 1\% or less of each hour in European frequency bands \cite{vangelista2015long}. For medium access control, LoRaWAN employs a Pure ALOHA-based approach where devices transmit whenever they have data, without prior channel sensing or coordination \cite{georgiou2017low}.

While these design choices significantly simplify device operation, reduce implementation complexity, and minimize power consumption, they also introduce fundamental challenges that become particularly acute in dense deployment scenarios. The ALOHA-based channel access mechanism inherently suffers from increased collision probability as network density grows, as devices transmit independently without awareness of concurrent transmissions from neighboring nodes \cite{bor2016lora}. The ADR mechanism, though effective in relatively static environments, may struggle to adapt quickly in highly dynamic conditions characterized by rapid changes in interference patterns, device mobility, or temporal variations in radio propagation \cite{slabicki2018adaptive}. Furthermore, the limited duty cycle constraints, while necessary for regulatory compliance and fair spectrum sharing, can exacerbate congestion issues by forcing devices to buffer data when the channel is unavailable, potentially leading to delayed or failed transmissions during peak traffic periods \cite{mikhaylov2016analysis}.

One of the most critical performance challenges in LoRaWAN deployments is packet loss, which directly impacts the reliability, data integrity, and timeliness of information delivery to applications. Packet loss fundamentally undermines the value proposition of IoT systems, as missed sensor readings can lead to incomplete datasets, delayed alerts, and suboptimal decision-making in time-critical applications \cite{centenaro2016long}. The causes of packet loss in LoRaWAN networks are multifaceted and often interdependent, stemming from a complex interplay of factors operating at different protocol layers and environmental scales.

Environmental dynamics play a significant role, with weather variations such as rain, fog, and temperature fluctuations affecting radio wave propagation through atmospheric attenuation and multipath fading \cite{petajajarvi2015performance}. Urban structures including buildings, vehicles, and vegetation create shadowing effects and non-line-of-sight conditions that can severely degrade signal strength, particularly in dense metropolitan areas \cite{liando2018real}. Radio frequency interference from other technologies operating in the same unlicensed ISM bands—most notably WiFi networks operating at 2.4 GHz, Bluetooth devices, and LTE systems—creates additional noise and can desensitize receivers or directly interfere with LoRa signals through intermodulation products \cite{croce2018impact}.

Network-level factors further compound these challenges. Device density in a given coverage area directly correlates with collision probability, as the ALOHA-based access scheme provides no collision avoidance mechanism \cite{bankov2016mathematical}. Mobility patterns of devices or obstacles in the radio environment can cause rapid variations in channel quality that the relatively slow ADR adaptation cannot track effectively \cite{cattani2017experimental}. Gateway capacity limitations, both in terms of processing capability and the number of simultaneous channels that can be monitored, can lead to packet drops during high-traffic periods even when packets successfully reach the gateway \cite{voigt2017mitigating}.

Empirical studies of real-world LoRaWAN deployments have documented packet loss rates ranging from as low as 8\% in optimal conditions to exceeding 60\% in challenged environments characterized by high device density, significant interference, or poor coverage \cite{MachineQ2025_LoRaWAN_Reliability, wixted2016evaluation}. Such high loss rates pose serious risks to IoT applications where reliable and timely data delivery is mission-critical. In industrial monitoring scenarios, lost sensor readings may delay the detection of equipment failures or hazardous conditions, potentially resulting in costly downtime or safety incidents \cite{abrardo2019optimization}. Smart city applications such as traffic management or environmental monitoring require consistent data streams to enable real-time decision-making and accurate trend analysis \cite{sanchez2020state}. In agricultural applications, missed soil moisture or weather data can lead to suboptimal irrigation schedules, affecting crop yields and water resource management \cite{ayoub2019internet}.

Conventional strategies for mitigating packet loss in LoRaWAN networks typically rely on retransmission mechanisms, redundancy-based schemes, or static optimization of transmission parameters. Retransmission protocols, such as confirmed mode transmissions where the network server acknowledges received packets, can improve reliability but at the cost of increased latency, higher energy consumption due to extended device wake times, and additional network congestion from acknowledgment traffic \cite{petajajarvi2017evaluation}. Fixed redundancy schemes that transmit duplicate packets over different channels or at different times can enhance delivery probability but significantly reduce spectral efficiency and limit network scalability by consuming additional airtime \cite{khaled2020lorawan}. Static spreading factor assignments or transmission power configurations may achieve good performance under specific conditions but fail to adapt to the dynamic nature of real-world deployments, leading to either unnecessary energy waste during favorable conditions or poor reliability when the environment degrades \cite{reynders2017improving}.

These limitations of traditional approaches highlight a fundamental trade-off between reliability, energy efficiency, and network capacity that has remained difficult to optimize using rule-based or static methods. There is a growing recognition in the research community that more sophisticated, adaptive, and context-aware solutions are needed to address the packet loss problem effectively \cite{haxhibeqiri2018survey}.

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{subsec:motivation}

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies over the past decade has fundamentally transformed numerous domains, demonstrating remarkable capabilities in pattern recognition, decision-making under uncertainty, and optimization of complex systems \cite{lecun2015deep}. These developments have naturally extended into the networking domain, where AI-based solutions are increasingly recognized not merely as experimental approaches but as practical tools capable of addressing longstanding challenges that resist traditional analytical solutions \cite{fadlullah2017state}. The question facing network researchers and practitioners is no longer whether AI will play a role in network optimization, but rather how to identify the specific problems that naturally align with the strengths and requirements of different AI paradigms.

Among the various machine learning frameworks, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as particularly promising for network optimization tasks due to its fundamental compatibility with the sequential, interactive nature of networking problems \cite{luong2019applications}. Reinforcement learning is specifically designed to solve problems that can be formulated as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), wherein an agent observes the current state of its environment, selects and executes an action, receives a scalar reward signal indicating the quality of that action, and transitions to a new state. Through repeated interactions, the agent learns a policy—a mapping from states to actions—that maximizes the expected cumulative reward over time \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. This framework maps remarkably well to many networking scenarios where systems must make sequential configuration decisions based on observable network conditions, with the goal of optimizing long-term performance metrics.

The challenge, then, is identifying which networking problems exhibit the structural characteristics that make them amenable to RL-based solutions. The LoRaWAN Adaptive Data Rate optimization problem in dense deployment scenarios represents a textbook example of such a problem, embodying all the key properties that suggest an RL approach may be superior to traditional methods.

Consider the fundamental challenge: LoRaWAN networks, particularly those supporting Industry 4.0 applications, smart factories, dense urban IoT deployments, and comprehensive environmental monitoring systems, face severe packet loss when many devices are concentrated within a relatively small geographic area, typically within a radius of 100 meters to 1 kilometer from the gateway \cite{reynders2018improving}. This packet loss stems from three primary mechanisms, each contributing to an environment of high uncertainty and dynamic interaction.

First, self-interference or intra-technology interference occurs when multiple LoRa devices transmit simultaneously using the same spreading factor on the same frequency channel \cite{croce2018impact}. The LoRa modulation scheme, while remarkably robust to noise and capable of demodulating signals below the noise floor, cannot reliably recover multiple overlapping transmissions that share the same spreading factor and frequency. When two or more packets collide in this manner—overlapping in both time and frequency domain—the typical outcome is the complete loss of all colliding packets, though in certain cases partial capture effects may allow the stronger signal to be decoded if the power imbalance is sufficient \cite{amaro2018understanding}.

Second, network congestion arises from the fundamental characteristics of the Pure ALOHA medium access control protocol employed by LoRaWAN \cite{georgiou2017low}. As device density increases, each device continues to transmit independently without any carrier sensing or coordination mechanism. The probability of collision grows exponentially with the number of active devices and their transmission frequency. In dense scenarios with 20 to 30 devices transmitting every 10 to 60 seconds within a confined area, the network effectively becomes a competitive game where each device's choice of transmission parameters—spreading factor, transmission power, frequency channel, and transmission timing—directly affects the collision probability and thus the success rate of all other devices in the network \cite{bankov2017should}.

Third, external interference from heterogeneous radio sources operating in the same unlicensed Industrial, Scientific, and Medical (ISM) bands creates an additional layer of unpredictability \cite{hosseinzadeh2020empirical}. WiFi networks operating at 2.4 GHz are ubiquitous in urban and industrial environments, and while LoRaWAN typically operates in sub-GHz bands (868 MHz in Europe, 915 MHz in North America), these WiFi devices can generate out-of-band emissions and intermodulation products that interfere with LoRa receivers. Similarly, cellular LTE devices carried by mobile users introduce time-varying interference patterns as people move through the environment, creating a dynamic interference landscape that changes on time scales of seconds to minutes. Bluetooth devices, ZigBee sensors, and other ISM band users further contribute to this complex electromagnetic environment.

The confluence of these factors creates a dynamic, uncertain, and highly interactive environment—precisely the conditions where RL excels. The problem exhibits all the hallmarks of a Markov Decision Process:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observable state}: Each device can measure relevant aspects of its environment, including Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) readings that capture current interference levels, Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) measurements indicating link quality, recent packet delivery rate tracking communication reliability, and battery state reflecting remaining energy reserves.
    
    \item \textbf{Actionable parameters}: Devices can modify their transmission configuration across multiple dimensions, including spreading factor selection (SF7 through SF12, with each step doubling airtime and improving sensitivity by approximately 2.5 dB), transmission power adjustment (typically from 2 dBm to 14 dBm in discrete steps), and frequency channel selection (with multiple 125 kHz channels available in the regulatory band).
    
    \item \textbf{Delayed rewards}: The consequences of parameter choices manifest over time through metrics such as successful packet delivery, energy consumption per successful transmission, and network-wide throughput, creating a classic RL reward structure.
    
    \item \textbf{Stochastic transitions}: The next state depends partially on the agent's action but also on uncontrollable factors including actions of other devices, movement of mobile interferers, and environmental variations, embodying the stochastic transition dynamics central to MDPs.
\end{itemize}

Furthermore, in dense deployments, the problem transcends a simple MDP and becomes a Markov Game or stochastic game, wherein multiple agents (devices) simultaneously learn and adapt their policies \cite{littman1994markov}. Each device's action affects not only its own performance but also the collision probability and achievable performance of other devices in the network. When Device A switches to spreading factor SF7 on channel 0, it directly impacts Device B if B is also configured to use SF7 on channel 0, as their transmissions will now collide with higher probability. This multi-agent interaction creates a complex strategic landscape where coordination and distributed optimization become crucial.

To appreciate why RL is the appropriate paradigm for this problem, it is instructive to consider the alternative of prediction-based approaches using supervised machine learning. Suppose we develop a sophisticated prediction model using recurrent neural networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, or Random Forest classifiers that can predict, with high accuracy based on current measurements and historical patterns, whether a forthcoming transmission is likely to experience a packet loss \cite{rizzi2017evaluation}. Such a model might achieve impressive accuracy rates of 85\% or even 90\% in forecasting transmission failures. However, this prediction capability, while valuable, is fundamentally incomplete. Knowing that a packet is likely to be lost does not, by itself, prevent that loss. The system requires an additional decision layer that can act on the prediction—adjusting the spreading factor to increase robustness, boosting transmission power to improve SNR, switching to a less congested channel, or deferring transmission to a more favorable time \cite{abdelfadeel2020machine}.

This is precisely where reinforcement learning provides its crucial advantage: RL does not merely predict outcomes but learns optimal actions in response to observed states \cite{mnih2015human}. An RL agent learns through trial and error which spreading factor, transmission power, and channel combinations yield the best long-term performance under various interference conditions, battery states, and network congestion levels. It discovers strategies such as "when pre-transmission RSSI exceeds -100 dBm and recent PDR is below 80\%, increase spreading factor by one step and consider switching to the least congested channel" without requiring explicit programming of such rules. The learning process naturally balances competing objectives—maximizing packet delivery rate while minimizing energy consumption—through the design of an appropriately structured reward function.

It is important to note that the relevance of RL-based ADR optimization is context-dependent and particularly applicable to dense, high-traffic deployment scenarios that are increasingly common in Industry 4.0 applications \cite{li2018internet}. When devices are sparsely deployed—separated by distances exceeding 1 kilometer from the gateway—and transmit infrequently (e.g., environmental sensors reporting once every 15 minutes or hourly), the problem characteristics change fundamentally. In such sparse networks, packet losses predominantly result from path loss effects and insufficient signal strength at the receiver rather than from collisions with other devices. Device interactions are minimal, collisions are rare events rather than common occurrences, and the environment is relatively static and predictable over the time scales relevant for ADR adaptation. Under these conditions, the classical LoRaWAN ADR algorithm, which gradually adjusts parameters based on received SNR margin, performs reasonably well and may be sufficient \cite{reynders2017power}.

However, the trend toward denser, more dynamic IoT deployments—driven by smart factory requirements for comprehensive sensor coverage, smart city initiatives deploying thousands of devices per square kilometer, and precision agriculture systems with dense sensor meshes—creates operating conditions where classical approaches become inadequate \cite{farooq2019survey}. In these dense, frequent-transmission scenarios characteristic of modern Industry 4.0 applications, intelligent, adaptive, and coordination-aware solutions are essential. Reinforcement learning, particularly multi-agent RL approaches that enable distributed coordination among devices, offers a principled framework for addressing these challenges \cite{busoniu2008comprehensive}.

The motivation for this research stems directly from these observations: to develop, implement, and rigorously evaluate RL-based approaches for ADR optimization in dense LoRaWAN deployments, demonstrating both the feasibility and the substantial performance improvements achievable through intelligent, learning-based network management.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{subsec:problem_statement}

Despite the widespread adoption of LoRaWAN technology in IoT applications and the theoretical promise of its Adaptive Data Rate mechanism for optimizing network performance, existing ADR implementations exhibit severe performance degradation in dense deployment scenarios that are increasingly common in modern smart infrastructure applications. This research addresses the critical problem of high packet loss rates and suboptimal energy efficiency in LoRaWAN networks operating under dense device deployment conditions, where traditional ADR approaches fail to adequately manage the complex, dynamic interactions among devices competing for limited radio resources.

Specifically, the problem manifests in several concrete dimensions:

\textbf{Inadequate packet delivery rates}: In dense deployments with 20-30 devices transmitting at intervals of 10-60 seconds within a radius of 100-500 meters, existing LoRaWAN networks employing classical ADR mechanisms achieve packet delivery rates as low as 25-40\%, with some devices experiencing near-complete communication failure \cite{bor2016lora}. This represents an unacceptable level of unreliability for mission-critical IoT applications in industrial monitoring, smart city infrastructure, and real-time environmental sensing.

\textbf{High variance in device-level performance}: Beyond aggregate network performance metrics, there exists substantial performance inequality across devices within the same network, with classical ADR producing standard deviations in packet delivery rate exceeding 40\%, meaning some devices achieve reasonable reliability while others are effectively disconnected \cite{slabicki2018adaptive}. This lack of fairness and predictability undermines the utility of the network for applications requiring consistent data collection from all sensors.

\textbf{Inefficient energy utilization}: When ADR mechanisms do respond to poor link conditions, they often resort to aggressive parameter adjustments—maximizing spreading factor and transmission power—that, while potentially improving immediate packet delivery, result in energy consumption levels 400-500\% higher than baseline configurations \cite{reynders2017power}. Given that many LoRaWAN devices are battery-powered with expected lifetimes of 2-10 years, such energy inefficiency directly translates to reduced operational lifetime and increased maintenance costs.

\textbf{Lack of coordination and collision awareness}: Classical ADR operates on each device independently based solely on locally observed link quality metrics (primarily SNR) without any awareness of network-level congestion, interference from other devices, or coordination of spreading factor and channel usage across the network \cite{cuomo2017explora}. This myopic, device-centric optimization can lead to convergence of multiple devices onto the same spreading factor and channel combination, actually exacerbating collision problems rather than alleviating them.

\textbf{Slow adaptation to dynamic conditions}: The gradual, incremental nature of classical ADR parameter adjustments means that adaptation lags significantly behind rapidly changing interference conditions, particularly those caused by mobile interferers such as WiFi-enabled smartphones or LTE devices carried by people moving through the coverage area \cite{mikhaylov2016analysis}. By the time ADR responds to degraded conditions, the interference pattern may have already shifted, rendering the adjustment ineffective or even counterproductive.

\textbf{Inability to proactively avoid interference}: Reactive ADR mechanisms respond to poor performance only after packet losses have already occurred, lacking any predictive or proactive capability to anticipate and avoid degraded channel conditions before transmission attempts \cite{abdelfadeel2020machine}. This reactive approach guarantees that devices will experience packet losses during the learning phase of adaptation, missing time-sensitive data that cannot be recovered retroactively.

The research problem can be formally stated as follows: \textit{How can we design, implement, and validate adaptive data rate mechanisms for LoRaWAN networks that dynamically optimize transmission parameters (spreading factor, transmission power, channel selection) to simultaneously maximize packet delivery rate and minimize energy consumption in dense deployment scenarios characterized by high device density, frequent transmissions, time-varying interference from heterogeneous sources, and complex multi-device interactions, while maintaining computational feasibility for resource-constrained IoT devices?}

This problem is particularly challenging because it requires balancing multiple competing objectives across different time scales, managing stochastic environmental dynamics and multi-agent interactions, learning effective policies from limited and noisy feedback signals, ensuring distributed coordination without excessive communication overhead, and maintaining practical implementability on low-power, computationally constrained embedded systems.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
\label{subsec:research_objectives}

This research aims to develop and validate reinforcement learning-based Adaptive Data Rate optimization mechanisms for LoRaWAN networks operating in dense deployment scenarios. The specific objectives are organized into theoretical, algorithmic, experimental, and practical implementation dimensions:

\subsubsection{Theoretical Formulation Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{MDP Formulation}: Formally model the single-device ADR parameter optimization problem as a Markov Decision Process, defining comprehensive state spaces that capture relevant network and link quality measurements (RSSI, SNR, packet delivery rate, energy state), action spaces spanning spreading factor, transmission power, and channel selection, and reward functions that appropriately balance packet delivery reliability against energy efficiency \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.
    
    \item \textbf{Markov Game Extension}: Extend the single-agent MDP formulation to a multi-agent Markov Game framework that captures the strategic interactions among multiple devices competing for shared spectrum resources, incorporating coordination signals and distributed state information to enable cooperative optimization \cite{littman1994markov}.
    
    \item \textbf{Reward Function Design}: Design congestion-adaptive reward functions that dynamically adjust optimization priorities based on network conditions, emphasizing packet delivery in high-interference scenarios while promoting energy efficiency under favorable conditions, thereby achieving context-appropriate optimization objectives \cite{gronauer2022multi}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Algorithm Development Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{DDQN-PER Implementation}: Develop a Double Deep Q-Network with Prioritized Experience Replay (DDQN-PER) agent tailored to the LoRaWAN ADR problem, incorporating domain-specific enhancements such as spreading factor ceiling constraints based on SNR requirements and dueling network architectures for improved value function approximation \cite{van2016deep, schaul2015prioritized}.
    
    \item \textbf{PPO Agent Design}: Implement a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent that leverages continuous action space representations for fine-grained transmission power control while maintaining discrete spreading factor selection, using actor-critic architectures and generalized advantage estimation for stable policy improvement \cite{schulman2017proximal}.
    
    \item \textbf{MARL Coordination Mechanism}: Develop a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) approach that enables distributed coordination among devices through coordination-aware action selection, channel congestion tracking, and implicit cooperation via shared learning signals, without requiring extensive inter-device communication \cite{busoniu2008comprehensive}.
    
    \item \textbf{Exploration Strategy Optimization}: Design adaptive exploration strategies that balance the exploration-exploitation tradeoff appropriately for network optimization, using techniques such as epsilon-greedy with exponential decay, coordination-biased exploration in multi-agent settings, and experience replay for sample efficiency \cite{mnih2015human}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Simulation and Validation Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Realistic Simulation Environment}: Construct a comprehensive simulation environment based on the ns-3 network simulator with accurate LoRaWAN physical and MAC layer models, incorporating realistic interference sources (WiFi, LTE mobile devices), path loss models (log-distance with shadowing), and urban obstacle modeling \cite{bianchi2019experimental}.
    
    \item \textbf{Comparative Performance Evaluation}: Conduct systematic experiments comparing RL-based ADR approaches (DDQN-PER, PPO, MARL) against baseline methods (no ADR, classical LoRaWAN ADR) across diverse scenarios varying in device density (10-30 devices), deployment radius (100-500 meters), transmission frequency (10-60 second periods), and interference conditions \cite{reynders2018improving}.
    
    \item \textbf{Multi-Metric Assessment}: Evaluate performance across multiple critical metrics including packet delivery rate (mean and standard deviation across devices), total energy consumption (mJ per device), energy efficiency (energy per successfully delivered packet), adaptation speed (time to converge to stable performance), and fairness (variance in performance across devices) \cite{slabicki2018adaptive}.
    
    \item \textbf{Scalability Analysis}: Investigate how the proposed RL-based approaches scale with increasing network size and density, identifying potential limitations and characterizing performance degradation curves as the problem complexity grows \cite{bankov2017should}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Physical Implementation Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hardware Testbed Development}: Design and construct a physical LoRaWAN testbed using resource-constrained IoT hardware (ESP32 microcontrollers with SX1278 LoRa transceivers) to validate the practical feasibility of deploying RL agents on embedded systems \cite{hosseinzadeh2020empirical}.
    
    \item \textbf{Real-World Validation}: Conduct experiments in realistic deployment environments to validate that the performance improvements observed in simulation translate to actual hardware operating under real-world interference and propagation conditions \cite{liando2018real}.
    
    \item \textbf{Computational Feasibility Assessment}: Measure and characterize the computational requirements of RL agent inference on resource-constrained devices, including memory footprint, processing latency, and additional energy overhead, ensuring that the benefits of intelligent ADR outweigh the implementation costs \cite{farooq2019survey}.
    
    \item \textbf{Integration Validation}: Demonstrate successful integration of RL-based ADR with standard LoRaWAN protocol implementations, including MAC command handling, downlink message processing, and compliance with LoRaWAN Alliance specifications \cite{adelantado2017understanding}.
\end{enumerate}

The successful achievement of these objectives will result in a comprehensive framework for RL-based ADR optimization in LoRaWAN networks, validated through both extensive simulation studies and physical testbed experiments, with demonstrated improvements in packet delivery rate, energy efficiency, and network fairness compared to existing approaches.

\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{subsec:research_gap}

While existing literature addresses various aspects of LoRaWAN optimization and machine learning applications in wireless networks, significant gaps remain in the application of reinforcement learning to dense LoRaWAN deployment scenarios. This section identifies the key research gaps that motivate the current work.

\subsubsection{Limited RL Application to LoRaWAN ADR}

The majority of existing LoRaWAN research focuses on analytical modeling, simulation-based parameter optimization, or static configuration strategies \cite{reynders2017improving, cuomo2017explora}. While several studies have explored machine learning for LoRaWAN, most employ supervised learning for prediction tasks (traffic forecasting, collision prediction, link quality estimation) rather than reinforcement learning for decision-making and control \cite{abdelfadeel2020machine, rizzi2017evaluation}. The few existing RL applications to LoRaWAN have primarily addressed single-agent scenarios or have not comprehensively addressed the dense deployment regime where multi-device interactions dominate performance \cite{hu2020machine}.

\subsubsection{Inadequate Modeling of Multi-Agent Interactions}

Previous research on LoRaWAN ADR optimization has largely treated devices as independent entities, optimizing each device's parameters in isolation without considering the coupled dynamics and strategic interactions among multiple co-located devices \cite{slabicki2018adaptive}. This independent optimization approach fundamentally misses the game-theoretic nature of dense deployments, where one device's choice of spreading factor and channel directly impacts collision probability and achievable performance for other devices. Multi-agent reinforcement learning frameworks that enable distributed coordination while respecting the computational and communication constraints of IoT devices remain largely unexplored in the LoRaWAN context \cite{busoniu2008comprehensive}.

\subsubsection{Insufficient Integration of Heterogeneous Interference Sources}

While interference has been recognized as a critical factor affecting LoRaWAN performance, most studies focus on intra-network interference (collisions among LoRa devices) or consider only a single external interference source \cite{croce2018impact}. Real-world deployments, particularly in urban and industrial environments, face complex heterogeneous interference from multiple technologies—WiFi, Bluetooth, LTE, other LoRa networks—operating simultaneously with varying temporal and spatial characteristics \cite{hosseinzadeh2020empirical}. Existing ADR approaches, both classical and learning-based, have not adequately addressed how to learn robust policies under such diverse and dynamic interference conditions.

\subsubsection{Energy Efficiency as a Secondary Concern}

While energy efficiency is frequently cited as a primary advantage of LoRaWAN and a key design consideration, many existing optimization approaches treat energy consumption as a secondary metric or constraint rather than co-optimizing it with packet delivery rate \cite{reynders2017power}. This is particularly problematic because naive approaches to improving reliability—such as maximizing spreading factor or transmission power—can dramatically increase energy consumption, potentially by factors of 4-5×, thereby negating the battery lifetime advantages that make LoRaWAN attractive for IoT applications \cite{khaled2020lorawan}. Methods that achieve high packet delivery rates while simultaneously maintaining low energy consumption remain an open challenge.

\subsubsection{Lack of Coordination Mechanisms}

Existing distributed optimization approaches for LoRaWAN largely assume that devices operate without coordination, making decisions based solely on locally observable information \cite{bankov2017should}. While fully centralized approaches with complete global state information have been explored in theoretical studies, they are impractical for large-scale deployments due to communication overhead, privacy concerns, and scalability limitations \cite{slabicki2018adaptive}. There is a notable gap in developing practical coordination mechanisms that enable implicit cooperation among devices—such as through distributed channel selection and spreading factor diversification—without requiring excessive signaling or centralized control.

\subsubsection{Limited Physical Validation}

A substantial portion of LoRaWAN optimization research relies exclusively on simulation studies or analytical models, with limited validation on physical hardware in realistic deployment conditions \cite{bianchi2019experimental}. While simulation is essential for exploring large parameter spaces and conducting controlled experiments, the gap between simulation assumptions and real-world radio propagation, hardware impairments, and implementation constraints can be significant \cite{liando2018real}. There is a need for research that bridges the simulation-to-reality gap, demonstrating that RL-based approaches can be successfully implemented on resource-constrained embedded systems and achieve promised performance improvements in practical deployments.

\subsubsection{Absence of Congestion-Adaptive Strategies}

Classical ADR operates with fixed decision logic regardless of network congestion levels, applying the same optimization strategy whether the network has 5 devices or 50 devices, whether traffic is sparse or dense \cite{cuomo2017explora}. This one-size-fits-all approach is inherently suboptimal, as the relative importance of different optimization objectives (reliability vs. energy efficiency vs. latency) naturally shifts with network conditions. Learning-based approaches that dynamically adapt their optimization priorities based on observed congestion levels represent an unexplored avenue for performance improvement.

\subsubsection{Scalability Concerns}

As LoRaWAN networks grow to serve hundreds or thousands of devices, questions of scalability become paramount. Most existing optimization approaches have been evaluated on small networks (typically fewer than 20 devices), and their performance characteristics at scale remain unclear \cite{reynders2018improving}. Whether RL-based approaches can maintain their performance advantages as network size increases, how to handle the exponential growth in state-action space complexity, and what modifications may be necessary for very large-scale deployments are open questions.

The current research addresses these gaps by: (1) developing and comparing multiple RL algorithms (value-based DDQN-PER, policy-based PPO, multi-agent MARL) specifically tailored to dense LoRaWAN scenarios; (2) incorporating comprehensive modeling of heterogeneous interference sources and multi-agent interactions; (3) explicitly co-optimizing packet delivery and energy efficiency through carefully designed reward functions; (4) implementing practical coordination mechanisms that enable distributed cooperation without excessive overhead; (5) validating approaches through both realistic simulations and physical testbed experiments; and (6) conducting scalability analysis across varying network sizes and density conditions.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:summary}

This research addresses the critical challenge of high packet loss and energy inefficiency in dense LoRaWAN deployments by applying reinforcement learning to Adaptive Data Rate optimization. The work is motivated by the fundamental mismatch between classical ADR mechanisms—designed for static, sparse networks—and the dynamic, interactive environment created by dense IoT deployments characteristic of Industry 4.0 applications.

The core contribution lies in formulating LoRaWAN ADR optimization as both a Markov Decision Process (for single-agent learning) and a Markov Game (for multi-agent coordination), enabling the application of advanced RL algorithms to learn policies that simultaneously optimize packet delivery rate and energy consumption. Three distinct RL approaches are developed and evaluated: Double Deep Q-Network with Prioritized Experience Replay (DDQN-PER) for robust value-based learning, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for stable policy gradient optimization with continuous action spaces, and Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) incorporating coordination mechanisms for distributed optimization.

Through comprehensive evaluation in realistic ns-3 simulations incorporating WiFi and LTE interference, urban obstacles, and mobile users, this research demonstrates that RL-based ADR can achieve packet delivery rates exceeding 99\% in scenarios where classical ADR achieves only 25-40\%, while simultaneously maintaining energy consumption comparable to or lower than baseline approaches. Physical testbed validation using ESP32 and SX1278 hardware confirms the practical feasibility of deploying these RL agents on resource-constrained IoT devices.

The research fills critical gaps in existing literature by: comprehensively addressing multi-agent interactions in dense deployments, integrating heterogeneous interference modeling, co-optimizing reliability and energy efficiency, developing practical coordination mechanisms for distributed learning, and validating approaches through physical implementation. The findings have significant implications for the design and deployment of reliable, energy-efficient LoRaWAN networks in smart cities, industrial IoT, and other applications requiring dense sensor coverage with dependable data collection.

\section{Literature Review}

This literature review examines the evolution of Adaptive Data Rate (ADR) mechanisms in LoRaWAN networks, from classical rule-based approaches to modern machine learning and reinforcement learning solutions. We begin with an overview of LoRaWAN technology and architecture, then explore the fundamental mechanisms of packet loss in dense IoT deployments. Following this, we review traditional ADR approaches and their limitations, before examining how machine learning, deep learning, and reinforcement learning techniques address these challenges in dynamic network environments.

\subsection{LoRaWAN Technology Overview}

LoRaWAN has emerged as a prominent Low Power Wide Area Network (LPWAN) technology for Internet of Things (IoT) deployments, offering long-range communication with minimal energy consumption \cite{Raza17, Augustin16}. The technology has been widely studied across both academic and real-world deployments to understand its performance characteristics, particularly regarding packet delivery reliability \cite{Liu-characterizing, Blenn-lorawan-in-the-wild}. Real-world datasets like the Oulu Smart Campus \cite{Eldeeb-lorawan} and empirical analyses from The Things Network have emphasized the unpredictability of packet delivery in urban and campus-scale environments, influenced by network density, environmental interference, and MAC-layer collisions.

\subsubsection{LoRa Modulation and Physical Layer}

LoRa (Long Range) constitutes the foundational radio frequency (RF) modulation technology that defines the physical layer for long-range communications in LoRaWAN. It is a proprietary physical layer modulation technique based on Chirp Spread Spectrum (CSS) modulation \cite{Augustin16}. This choice of modulation enables high sensitivity, allowing gateways to recover weak signals and achieve increased coverage even at lower data rates. The robust noise tolerance characteristic of LoRa technology allows signals to be received below the noise floor \cite{Ruan2025_LoRa_Localization}.

Building upon the LoRa physical layer, LoRaWAN functions as the Media Access Control (MAC) layer protocol that defines how devices use the LoRa hardware, including transmission timing and message formats. This protocol stack provides essential features such as adaptive data rate (ADR), bi-directional communication capabilities, and robust security mechanisms \cite{Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML}.

Key parameters within the LoRa physical layer include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spreading Factor (SF)}: The SF determines the rate at which chirps are transmitted and directly influences the data rate and the Time-on-Air (ToA) for a packet. A higher SF increases the signal's resistance to noise and interference, extending the communication range. However, it also increases the packet's ToA, which raises device energy consumption and collision probability.
    
    \item \textbf{Bandwidth (BW)}: The BW specifies the width of the frequency band used for the chirp. It is inversely proportional to the ToA; a wider bandwidth results in shorter ToA but reduces the range and sensitivity of the link.
    
    \item \textbf{Coding Rate (CR)}: The CR adds redundant bits to the packet, providing forward error correction (FEC) that makes transmission more resilient to burst interference at the cost of increasing ToA.
\end{itemize}

Link quality in LoRaWAN is primarily measured by two metrics: Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI), which measures total received power including signal, noise, and interference; and Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), which quantifies the ratio of signal power to noise power, indicating link quality and the likelihood of successful packet reception \cite{GonzalezPalacio2024_ML_TransmissionPowerControl, Aarif2025rssi_prediction}.

\subsubsection{LoRaWAN Network Architecture}

The LoRaWAN architecture employs a star-of-stars topology consisting of four main components: end devices, gateways, a network server, and an application server \cite{Raza17, Augustin16}. The architecture is designed to support long-range, low-power communication by keeping end devices as simple and energy-efficient as possible, shifting network complexity to the central server.


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{lora network architecture.png}
    \caption{LoRa Network Architecture \cite{Augustin16}}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}


\textbf{End Devices} are sensor nodes that collect data and transmit wirelessly using LoRa modulation to one or more gateways. They are typically low-cost, battery-powered, and designed for long-term operation. Energy consumption is significantly influenced by transmission time-on-air, with battery life ranging from one to five years depending on sensing intervals \cite{alkhayyal2025enhancing_lorawan_sensor_networks}.

\textbf{Gateways} (also known as concentrators) serve as bridges between end devices and the network server. A single gateway can simultaneously receive messages from thousands of end devices \cite{Blenn-lorawan-in-the-wild}. They receive LoRa packets from end devices, convert them to IP packets, and forward them to the network server via standard backhaul connections. Multi-gateway reception, where a single end device can be heard by multiple gateways, improves network reliability and range \cite{Guerrero-towards-dependable}.

\textbf{Network Server} is the central brain of the LoRaWAN network \cite{Raza17, Augustin16}, responsible for packet management (including deduplication of packets from multiple gateways \cite{Guerrero-towards-dependable}), routing, ADR control, and security. The network server implements the ADR algorithm, dynamically adjusting data rate and transmission power of each end device to optimize battery life and network capacity \cite{alGumaei2022ANovelApproach, TheThingsIndustriesADR}.

\textbf{Application Server} hosts the end-user application, handling payload encryption/decryption and data processing for specific applications \cite{Raza17}.

\subsubsection{LoRaWAN Device Classes}

LoRaWAN defines three device classes to address varying application requirements concerning power consumption and downlink communication latency \cite{Raza17, Augustin16}:

\textbf{Class A} is the most power-efficient class and mandatory for all LoRaWAN devices. Devices only open two short receive windows after uplink transmissions, remaining in deep sleep otherwise. This asynchronous communication makes Class A ideal for applications primarily sending small amounts of data without requiring immediate downlink communication \cite{Raza17, alkhayyal2025enhancing_lorawan_sensor_networks}.

\textbf{Class B} provides scheduled receive windows synchronized by gateway beacon signals, in addition to Class A receive windows. This enables predictable downlink message delivery suitable for applications like remote configuration updates while maintaining reasonable energy efficiency \cite{Raza17}.

\textbf{Class C} offers continuous receive windows, making it the least power-efficient but providing lowest latency for downlink communication. This class suits applications requiring constant monitoring and real-time control, such as smart street lighting or industrial control systems \cite{Raza17, leonardi}.

\subsection{Mechanisms of Packet Loss in LoRaWAN}

Packet loss is an inevitable characteristic of wireless transmission environments. Understanding the multifaceted mechanisms contributing to packet loss is crucial for developing effective prediction and mitigation strategies \cite{zheng2025effects}. Measurement-based studies show that a significant number of packet losses occur even when RSSI and SNR values are sufficiently high, indicating that congestion and collisions are dominant loss factors in dense networks \cite{Liu-characterizing}.

\subsubsection{Collisions and MAC Layer Limitations}

LoRaWAN primarily employs a pure ALOHA protocol for uplink transmissions, where end devices transmit data whenever available without prior coordination or channel sensing. While this simplicity reduces device complexity and power consumption, it inherently leads to increased collisions as network density grows, significantly limiting scalability \cite{Guerrero-towards-dependable}. When multiple devices transmit simultaneously or external interference causes concurrent transmission overlap, packets collide and become undecipherable at the gateway, forcing retransmissions.

The fundamental reliance on pure ALOHA inherently limits LoRaWAN scalability in dense deployments. This problem is compounded by late or lost packets consuming airtime that could be utilized by other devices, leading to decreased network throughput and increased latency. Retransmission attempts due to collisions result in higher energy consumption, negating LoRaWAN's key advantage of long battery life \cite{MachineQ2025_LoRaWAN_Reliability}. This creates a vicious cycle where congestion causes packet loss, which triggers more retransmissions, further increasing congestion. Accurate traffic prediction becomes crucial for breaking this cycle \cite{Tabatowski-Bush2024LoRaWANWarehouse}.

\subsubsection{Environmental Factors and Path Loss}

The wireless channel in LoRaWAN is highly susceptible to environmental factors that significantly influence signal propagation and packet delivery success \cite{Raza17}. As distance between end devices and gateways increases, signal strength diminishes, making transmissions more vulnerable to interference, physical obstacles, and varying environmental conditions \cite{Guo21, Bor16}.

Path loss (PL) and shadow fading are profoundly influenced by environmental variables including temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, and particulate matter \cite{rademacher2021pathlossurbanlora, Mokua2025A_Comprehensive_data}. Empirical studies have highlighted barometric pressure as a notably influential environmental factor affecting path loss across various models \cite{Mokua2025A_Comprehensive_data, Aarif2025rssi_prediction}. Accurate path loss prediction incorporating real-time environmental data is essential for optimizing LoRaWAN performance. The availability of rich datasets including environmental variables, such as the LoRaWAN Path Loss Dataset from Medellín, Colombia \cite{alkhayyal2025enhancing_lorawan_sensor_networks} and the Oulu Smart Campus Dataset \cite{Eldeeb-lorawan}, enables development of more nuanced and accurate packet loss prediction models \cite{GonzalezPalacio2024_ML_TransmissionPowerControl}.

\subsubsection{Device Behavior and Network Congestion}

Individual device behavior and aggregate network traffic play critical roles in determining packet delivery success and overall system performance. Network traffic, dynamically regulated by the number of active users and their transmission patterns, is a key determinant of system optimization and packet delivery rates \cite{Tabatowski-Bush2024LoRaWANWarehouse}. In environments with large numbers of connections, congestion leads to increased energy resource usage in constrained devices, reducing throughput and increasing latency \cite{rahman2023enhancing}.

The interplay between individual device behavior (varying packet rates, different operation modes, data criticality) and aggregate network congestion creates a dynamic feedback loop. Devices experiencing packet loss often retry transmissions, further contributing to congestion and creating cascading performance degradation \cite{Imo2025}. This highlights the need for predictive models that can anticipate traffic peaks and proactively adjust device parameters rather than react to congestion \cite{Tabatowski-Bush2024LoRaWANWarehouse}.

\subsection{Traditional Adaptive Data Rate Mechanisms}

The Adaptive Data Rate (ADR) mechanism is a core feature of LoRaWAN, designed to optimize energy consumption and network throughput by adjusting transmission parameters such as spreading factor (SF) and transmission power (TP) \cite{Kufakunesu2020, Kufakunesu2020}.

\subsubsection{Classical ADR Operation}

Traditional ADR algorithms operate on a feedback control principle, where the Network Server analyzes received signal quality metrics such as RSSI and SNR from recent uplink transmissions to make parameter adjustments \cite{alGumaei2022ANovelApproach}. The algorithm typically maintains a margin-based approach, calculating the difference between measured SNR and minimum required SNR for reliable demodulation. When sufficient margin exists, the algorithm increases data rate (decreases SF) to improve network capacity and reduce airtime, thereby reducing collision probability.

The Things Stack ADR implementation examines SNR to determine data rate and transmission power, analyzes frame counters to determine packet loss, and sets the number of retransmissions accordingly \cite{TheThingsIndustriesADR}. The network server uses these metrics to determine if a device can communicate reliably at lower SF and higher data rate, shortening ToA and saving energy. Conversely, if link quality degrades, the server commands the device to use higher SF and increased power to ensure connectivity.

\subsubsection{Limitations of Classical ADR}

While classical ADR performs well in static and low-density deployments, several studies have shown major limitations in dynamic, mobile, or dense network environments \cite{Kufakunesu2020, Kufakunesu2020}. Its rule-based operation relies on long-term averages of link quality indicators, resulting in slow adaptation to changing channel conditions. Classical ADR often converges slowly, allocates suboptimal data rates, and increases packet loss due to interference and congestion \cite{FarhadPyun2023, Zholamanov2024, Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML}.

A critical limitation is that classical ADR cannot distinguish between packet losses caused by weak links and those caused by collisions, leading to inappropriate parameter adjustments \cite{Liu-characterizing}. In dense networks, measurement-based studies show significant packet losses occur even with sufficiently high RSSI and SNR values, indicating congestion and collisions as dominant loss factors. Traditional ADR assumes static or slowly varying channel conditions, making it suboptimal for mobile applications where channel conditions change rapidly \cite{alGumaei2022ANovelApproach}.

This limitation has motivated the development of congestion-aware ADR algorithms that evaluate spreading factor congestion status and adjust allocations accordingly, demonstrating improved data extraction rates and energy consumption performance \cite{Finnegan2020adr}. Enhanced ADR variants such as Blind ADR (BADR) for mobile scenarios, ADR+, Rel-ADR, and MB-ADR rely on averaged or median SNR measurements to stabilize parameter selection \cite{Finnegan2020adr}. Collision Avoidance ADR enforces SF selection based on receiver sensitivity thresholds to prevent unstable configurations \cite{Kufakunesu2024}.

\subsection{Machine Learning and Deep Learning Approaches for ADR}

To address the challenges of classical ADR, machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and reinforcement learning (RL) techniques have been increasingly explored for intelligent resource management in LoRaWAN networks \cite{FarhadPyun2023, Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML, Finnegan2020adr, Djoudi2022mdp}. ML-based approaches enable dynamic optimization of physical layer parameters such as SF, TP, bandwidth (BW), and coding rate (CR), improving packet success ratio (PSR), reducing energy consumption, and mitigating interference \cite{Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML}.

\subsubsection{Supervised Learning Techniques}

Supervised learning techniques have been widely applied to configure physical layer parameters and predict packet collisions or energy consumption. Models such as Multiple Linear Regression, Gaussian Naive Bayes, Decision Trees, Random Forests, and Support Vector Machines have been trained using metrics including RSSI, SNR, SF, and time-on-air to improve PSR and energy efficiency \cite{Zholamanov2024, Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML}.

Deep learning approaches, including Deep Neural Networks, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), further enhance performance by capturing temporal dependencies in traffic patterns and channel variations \cite{Finnegan2020adr}. LSTM networks, introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber \cite{Hochreiter97}, have proven particularly effective for time-series prediction in LoRaWAN contexts, enabling prediction of network traffic, path loss, and link quality degradation.

\subsubsection{Environment-Aware Optimization}

Several DL-based solutions focus on environment-aware optimization. DeepLoRa employs bidirectional LSTM models combined with satellite imagery to construct path loss models and improve land cover classification accuracy \cite{lui2021DeepLora}. AI-ERA introduces a DNN-based approach that predicts optimal data rates directly at the end device, achieving PSR improvements of over 25\% \cite{FarhadPyun2023}.

Beyond statistical and ML optimization, fuzzy logic approaches have been proposed to infer optimal ADR configurations with low computational overhead \cite{Kufakunesu2022}. Unsupervised learning techniques such as K-means clustering have been applied to group end devices based on transmission characteristics, enabling more efficient delay and energy management \cite{Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML, Cuomo2018towards}.

\subsubsection{Limitations of ML and DL Approaches}

Despite these benefits, many ML and DL approaches require substantial computational resources or depend on auxiliary information such as GPS data, making them less suitable for resource-constrained end devices \cite{Zholamanov2024, Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML, Farhad2022}. Most ML- and DL-based ADR solutions assume homogeneous device hardware and stable interference conditions. In practice, hardware variability including antenna gain differences and noise figure variations can significantly impact packet reception, particularly in dense deployments \cite{Zholamanov2024}.

Furthermore, many ML and DL methods rely on offline training with static datasets, limiting their ability to adapt to rapidly changing network conditions. In highly dynamic or congested environments, these models may struggle to generalize, motivating the use of online learning approaches such as reinforcement learning \cite{Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML}.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Dynamic Parameter Optimization}

Reinforcement learning has emerged as a promising alternative due to its ability to learn optimal policies through direct interaction with the network environment, without requiring labeled datasets \cite{Zholamanov2024, Djoudi2022mdp}. RL-based ADR approaches can continuously adapt transmission parameters in response to real-time feedback, making them well suited for dynamic and congested LoRaWAN deployments. In slotted LoRaWAN designs, RL has been applied to autonomously mitigate collisions and improve throughput using protocol-compliant mechanisms \cite{Slabicki2020adaptive}.

\subsubsection{Reward Function Design}

A critical challenge in RL-based ADR design is the formulation of the reward function. Improperly balanced rewards may cause RL agents to favor higher spreading factors, increasing airtime and collision probability rather than improving reliability \cite{Zholamanov2024}. Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB)-based approaches such as LP-MAB demonstrate the importance of balancing exploration, reliability, and energy efficiency under varying traffic and mobility conditions \cite{Djoudi2022mdp, Sandoval2020rl}.

\subsubsection{Q-Learning and Multi-Armed Bandit Approaches}

Q-learning has been applied to LoRaWAN ADR by modeling the gateway as an agent that optimizes transmission parameters based on packet reception outcomes, reporting improvements in packet delivery ratio (PDR) compared to random or classical ADR strategies \cite{Farhad23-LoRaWAN-Meets-ML, Sandoval2020rl}. MAB-based schemes optimize SF and TP selection by balancing exploration and exploitation \cite{Djoudi2022mdp, Sandoval2020rl}. MIX-MAB architectures combining exploration-exploitation strategies such as EXP3 have shown faster convergence and higher packet delivery performance \cite{Djoudi2022mdp}.

\subsubsection{Deep Reinforcement Learning}

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) extends classical RL by using neural networks to approximate value functions and policies. DQN-based methods have been proposed to optimize SF, TP, and channel selection, demonstrating improved PDR compared to traditional ADR algorithms \cite{Zhong2022drl, Sandoval2020rl, Milarokostas2024qlearning}. The foundational DQN algorithm \cite{mnih2015human} has been enhanced through techniques such as Double DQN \cite{van2016deep} and Prioritized Experience Replay \cite{schaul2015prioritized} to address overestimation bias and improve learning stability \cite{Zholamanov2024}.

Lightweight RL schemes jointly optimizing channel and spreading factor selection have been validated on real devices, offering low complexity solutions suitable for resource-limited nodes \cite{Slabicki2020adaptive}. Policy gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017proximal} have emerged as alternatives to value-based methods, offering more stable training in continuous action spaces.

\subsubsection{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning}

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has been proposed to address scalability and coordination challenges in dense LoRaWAN deployments \cite{Benkhelifa2020distributed}. MARL frameworks enable multiple agents to learn adaptive transmission policies while accounting for shared network resources \cite{Benkhelifa2020distributed}. Many MARL solutions adopt centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE), allowing agents to learn cooperative behaviors while operating independently.

\subsubsection{Key Optimization Considerations}

Several studies emphasize the importance of SNR-based decision-making for reliable link adaptation. Variants such as ADR+, Rel-ADR, and MB-ADR rely on averaged or median SNR measurements to stabilize parameter selection \cite{Finnegan2020adr}. Collision Avoidance ADR enforces SF selection based on receiver sensitivity thresholds to prevent unstable configurations \cite{Kufakunesu2024}. These SNR-based mechanisms act as safety constraints, particularly in low-congestion scenarios where learning-based approaches may otherwise increase spreading factors unnecessarily.

\subsection{Summary and Research Gaps}

This literature review has traced the evolution of ADR mechanisms in LoRaWAN from traditional rule-based approaches to advanced machine learning and reinforcement learning solutions. While classical ADR provides a foundation for resource optimization in LoRaWAN networks, it struggles in dense, dynamic, and mobile environments where collisions and congestion dominate packet loss mechanisms. Machine learning and deep learning approaches have demonstrated significant improvements in prediction accuracy and parameter optimization, but often require substantial computational resources and offline training that limits adaptability.

Reinforcement learning emerges as a particularly promising direction, offering online adaptation through direct environmental interaction without labeled datasets. Recent advances in Deep RL (DDQN-PER, PPO) and Multi-Agent RL (MARL) provide sophisticated frameworks for handling the complex, multi-device optimization challenges inherent in dense LoRaWAN deployments. However, significant research gaps remain in: (1) validating RL-based ADR approaches in realistic testbeds with actual hardware constraints, (2) developing lightweight RL algorithms suitable for resource-constrained end devices, (3) designing reward functions that properly balance packet delivery, energy efficiency, and collision avoidance, and (4) addressing the scalability challenges of MARL in very large-scale deployments with hundreds or thousands of devices.

The integration of environmental factors, traffic prediction, and congestion-aware decision-making represents a critical frontier for future ADR development. As LoRaWAN continues to scale for massive IoT deployments in smart cities, industrial automation, and precision agriculture, the need for intelligent, adaptive, and energy-efficient resource management becomes increasingly vital.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
% ============================================================================

This section presents our approach to optimizing Adaptive Data Rate (ADR) in LoRaWAN using reinforcement learning. We begin with the simulation environment setup, describe the baseline classical ADR algorithm, and then present our three RL-based approaches: DDQN-PER, PPO, and MARL.

\subsection{Simulation Environment}
\label{subsec:simulation_environment}

\subsubsection{ns-3 Network Simulator}

We build our simulation on top of the ns-3 discrete event network simulator, which is one of the most widely used open source network simulators in the research community. Specifically, we use ns-3.45 with the LoRaWAN module that provide accurate modeling of the LoRa physical layer and the LoRaWAN MAC layer protocol.

The ns-3 framework allow us to model the complete LoRaWAN protocol stack, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Physical layer with configurable spreading factors (SF7--SF12), bandwidth (125 kHz), and transmission power (2--14 dBm)
    \item LoRaWAN MAC layer with ALOHA like channel access
    \item Network server that collect data from gateways
    \item Propagation loss models for realistic signal attenuation
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Path Loss Model}

The signal propagation in our simulation follow the Log Distance Path Loss model, which is the standard model used for LoRaWAN network planning. The received signal power at distance $d$ from the transmitter is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    P_{rx}(d) = P_{tx} + G_{tx} + G_{rx} - PL(d)
    \label{eq:link_budget}
\end{equation}

where $P_{tx}$ is the transmission power in dBm, $G_{tx}$ and $G_{rx}$ are the antenna gains, and $PL(d)$ is the path loss given by:

\begin{equation}
    PL(d) = PL(d_0) + 10 \cdot n \cdot \log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{d_0}\right) + X_{\sigma}
    \label{eq:path_loss}
\end{equation}

Here, $PL(d_0)$ is the path loss at reference distance $d_0 = 1$\,m, $n$ is the path loss exponent (typically 2.5--3.5 for urban environment), and $X_{\sigma}$ is a zero mean Gaussian random variable with standard deviation $\sigma$ that represent shadow fading.

The Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR) at the receiver is then:

\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR} = P_{rx} - N_f - 10\log_{10}(kTB)
    \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}

where $N_f$ is the noise figure of the receiver, $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ is the temperature in Kelvin, and $B$ is the bandwidth.

Each spreading factor require a minimum SNR for successful demodulation. These sensitivity thresholds are:

\subsubsection{Interference Modeling}

On top of the standard ns-3 LoRaWAN module, we build additional interference modeling to create a more realistic environment:

\subsubsection{WiFi Interference}

WiFi devices operate in the 2.4\,GHz ISM band and can cause out of band interference to LoRa devices operating at 868\,MHz (EU) through intermodulation products and spurious emissions. In our simulation, we model WiFi interferers as stationary nodes randomly placed within 80\% of the deployment radius. The WiFi interference power vary with time to simulate traffic patterns:

\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{WiFi}}(t) = P_{\text{base}} + 10\log_{10}\left(0.5 + 0.5\sin\left(\frac{t}{30}\right)\right)
    \label{eq:wifi_interference}
\end{equation}

where $P_{\text{base}}$ is sampled uniformly from $[-130, -110]$\,dBm.

\subsubsection{LTE Mobile Interference}

We also model LTE mobile devices that represent people walking with smartphones. These are modeled using the ns-3 \texttt{RandomWalk2dMobilityModel}, which simulate human walking patterns with the following parameters:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Walking speed: uniformly distributed between 0.5\,m/s and 2.0\,m/s (corresponding to 1.8--7.2\,km/h)
    \item Direction change: every 50\,m of walking distance
    \item Device height: 1.5\,m (hand held position)
    \item Movement bounded within the deployment area
\end{itemize}

These LTE devices create time varying interference because they move through the network, causing different interference patterns at different times. This make the environment more dynamic and challenging for the ADR algorithm.

\subsubsection{Urban Obstacles}

The simulation also include virtual urban obstacles (buildings, walls) that cause additional signal attenuation. These are randomly placed within the deployment area and add 5--15\,dB of attenuation to signal paths that cross them.

\subsubsection{Pre-Transmission RSSI Sampling}

A key feature of our simulation is the pre-transmission RSSI sampling. Before each device transmit, it read the RSSI register to measure the current interference level at its location. The pre-TX RSSI is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{RSSI}_{\text{pre-tx}} = 10\log_{10}\left(\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} 10^{P_{i}/10} + 10^{N_0/10}\right) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{hw}}^2)
    \label{eq:pre_tx_rssi}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{I}$ is the set of active interferers (both LoRa and external), $P_i$ is the received power from each interferer, $N_0$ is the ambient noise floor, and $\sigma_{\text{hw}}$ model hardware measurement noise.

\subsubsection{Simulation Framework}

On top of the ns-3 engine, we created a simulation framework that allow easy experimentation. The framework accept the following parameters through command line:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{--nDevices}: Number of end devices (default: 10)
    \item \texttt{--simulationTime}: Total simulation time in seconds
    \item \texttt{--appPeriod}: Packet transmission period in seconds
    \item \texttt{--radius}: Deployment radius in meters
    \item \texttt{--adr}: ADR mode -- \texttt{off}, \texttt{on} (classical), \texttt{ddqn}, \texttt{ppo}, \texttt{marl}, or \texttt{all}
    \item \texttt{--wifiInterferers}: Number of WiFi interference sources
    \item \texttt{--lteInterferers}: Number of LTE mobile interference sources
\end{itemize}

When the \texttt{all} mode is selected, the framework automatically run all five ADR methods sequentially with the same random seed, same device positions, and same interference patterns, so the comparison is fair. The results are stored as per device CSV files in the \texttt{lorawan\_datasets/} folder.

\subsection{Classical ADR Algorithm}
\label{subsec:classical_adr}
\subsubsection{Why ADR Matters}

In a LoRaWAN network, each device can choose its spreading factor (SF7--SF12) and transmission power (2--14\,dBm). These two parameters have a direct tradeoff:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Higher SF}: Provide better receiver sensitivity (can decode weaker signals), but consume more energy and occupy the channel for longer time, which increase the probability of collisions.
    \item \textbf{Higher TX Power}: Increase the signal strength at the gateway but consume more energy.
    \item \textbf{Lower SF and Lower TX Power}: Save energy and reduce airtime (less collision chance), but only work when the link quality is good enough.
\end{itemize}

The purpose of ADR is to find the optimal balance: use the lowest SF and lowest TX power that still maintain reliable communication. In a static environment with few devices, this is straightforward. But in a dynamic, dense environment with interference, it become a challenging optimization problem.

\subsubsection{How Classical ADR Works}

The classical ADR algorithm as specified in the LoRaWAN 1.0.4 specification work as a centralized approach. The network server (connected to the gateway) collect SNR measurements from received packets and calculate the optimal parameters for each device.

The algorithm is as follow:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Classical LoRaWAN ADR Algorithm}
\label{alg:classical_adr_algorithm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require SNR measurement from received packet, current SF, current TP
\State $\text{SNR}_{\text{margin}} \gets \text{SNR}_{\text{measured}} - \text{SNR}_{\text{required}}[\text{SF}]$
\If{$\text{SNR}_{\text{margin}} > \text{SNR}_{\text{threshold}} + 3.0$ \textbf{and} $\text{SF} > 7$}
    \State $\text{SF} \gets \text{SF} - 1$ \Comment{Decrease SF -- better data rate}
\ElsIf{$\text{SNR}_{\text{margin}} < -\text{SNR}_{\text{threshold}}$ \textbf{and} $\text{SF} < 12$}
    \State $\text{SF} \gets \text{SF} + 1$ \Comment{Increase SF -- better sensitivity}
\EndIf
\If{$\text{SNR}_{\text{margin}} > \text{SNR}_{\text{threshold}} + 5.0$ \textbf{and} $\text{TP} > 2$}
    \State $\text{TP} \gets \max(2, \text{TP} - 2)$ \Comment{Reduce power -- save energy}
\ElsIf{$\text{SNR}_{\text{margin}} < -\text{SNR}_{\text{threshold}} - 2.0$ \textbf{and} $\text{TP} < 14$}
    \State $\text{TP} \gets \min(14, \text{TP} + 2)$ \Comment{Boost power -- improve link}
\EndIf
\State Apply updated SF and TP to device
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The SNR threshold used in our implementation is $\text{SNR}_{\text{threshold}} = 2.5$\,dB.

\subsubsection{Limitations of Classical ADR}

The classical ADR has several limitations that make it unsuitable for dense deployment:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reactive, not proactive}: It only adjust parameters after observing good or bad link quality. It cannot anticipate future conditions.
    \item \textbf{No interference awareness}: It does not consider the interference from other devices or external sources. It only look at the received SNR.
    \item \textbf{No coordination between devices}: Each device is adjusted independently without considering what other devices are doing. This can lead to situations where multiple devices converge to the same SF and channel, making collisions worse.
    \item \textbf{Slow adaptation}: The algorithm adjust parameters gradually, one step at a time. In a rapidly changing environment, this may be too slow.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Based ADR Optimization}
\label{subsec:rl_adr}
\subsubsection{Problem Formulation as MDP}

We model the parameter optimization problem for each LoRa device as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma)$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State space $\mathcal{S}$}: The state vector for each device at time $t$ is:
      \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        s_t = \Big[
        &\text{RSSI}_{\text{norm}},\;
        \sigma^2_{\text{RSSI}},\;
        \text{SNR}_{\text{norm}},\;
        \Delta\text{SNR},\;
        \text{PDR}_{\text{recent}},\;
        \Delta\text{PDR}, \\
        &\frac{L_{\text{consec}}}{5},\;
        \frac{t_{\text{idle}}}{300},\;
        \frac{\text{SF}-7}{5},\;
        \frac{\text{TP}-2}{12},\;
        \frac{c}{7},\;
        f_{\text{coord}}
        \Big]
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:state_vector}
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{RSSI}_{\text{norm}}$ is the normalized last RSSI reading, $\sigma^2_{\text{RSSI}}$ is the RSSI variance (indicates time-varying interference), $\text{SNR}_{\text{norm}}$ is the normalized last SNR, $\Delta\text{SNR}$ is the SNR trend, $\text{PDR}_{\text{recent}}$ is the recent packet delivery rate, $\Delta\text{PDR}$ is the PDR trend, $L_{\text{consec}}$ is consecutive packet losses, $t_{\text{idle}}$ is the time since last successful transmission, $c$ is the current channel, and $f_{\text{coord}}$ is the coordination feature (number of devices on same channel, used in MARL).

    \item \textbf{Action space $\mathcal{A}$}: The agent can choose from 48 discrete actions that combine:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Spreading factor adjustment: SF7 through SF12
        \item Transmission power levels: 8, 11, or 14\,dBm
        \item Channel change decisions
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Transition probability $\mathcal{P}$}: The state transition is determined by the wireless environment (channel conditions, interference, mobility of LTE devices), which is stochastic and not known to the agent.

    \item \textbf{Reward function $\mathcal{R}$}: Described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:reward}.

    \item \textbf{Discount factor $\gamma = 0.95$}: Balance between immediate and future rewards.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Reward Function}
\label{sec:reward}

The reward function is congestion-adaptive and consist of four components:

\begin{equation}
    r_t = w_1 \cdot R_{\text{outcome}} + w_2 \cdot R_{\text{interference}} + w_3 \cdot R_{\text{stability}} + w_4 \cdot R_{\text{efficiency}}
    \label{eq:reward_function}
\end{equation}

The weights are dynamically adjusted based on the estimated congestion level $C \in [0, 1]$:

\begin{align}
    w_1 &= 0.4 + 0.3(1 - C) & \text{(Packet outcome: 0.4--0.7)} \\
    w_2 &= 0.25 \cdot C & \text{(Interference avoidance: 0.0--0.25)} \\
    w_3 &= 0.2 + 0.15(1 - C) & \text{(PDR stability: 0.2--0.35)} \\
    w_4 &= 0.15 + 0.2(1 - C) & \text{(Energy efficiency: 0.15--0.35)}
\end{align}

The congestion level $C$ is estimated from observable metrics:

\begin{equation}
    C = f\left(\frac{N_{\text{devices}}}{T_{\text{period}}},\; \text{RSSI}_{\text{pre-tx}},\; \sigma^2_{\text{RSSI}},\; \text{PDR}_{\text{recent}}\right)
    \label{eq:congestion}
\end{equation}

where the traffic intensity $N_{\text{devices}}/T_{\text{period}}$ is the primary factor.

\subsubsection{Energy Consumption Calculation}

The energy consumed per packet transmission is calculated based on the LoRa physical layer parameters:

The symbol rate for a given spreading factor is:
\begin{equation}
    R_s = \frac{B}{2^{\text{SF}}}
    \label{eq:symbol_rate}
\end{equation}

where $B = 125$\,kHz is the bandwidth. The total number of symbols in a packet:
\begin{equation}
    N_{\text{sym}} = N_{\text{preamble}} + N_{\text{header}} + \max\left(0,\; \left\lceil \frac{8 \cdot L_{\text{payload}} - 4 \cdot \text{SF} + 28 + 16}{4 \cdot \text{SF}} \right\rceil \times 5 + 8\right)
    \label{eq:symbols}
\end{equation}

where $N_{\text{preamble}} = 8$ symbols, $N_{\text{header}} = 4.25$ symbols, and $L_{\text{payload}}$ is the payload size in bytes.

The time on air is:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{air}} = \frac{N_{\text{sym}}}{R_s} \quad \text{(seconds)}
    \label{eq:toa}
\end{equation}

The transmit current consumption is modeled as a linear function of TX power (based on SX1276 datasheet):
\begin{equation}
    I_{tx} = 85 + 2.3 \cdot (P_{tx} - 2) \quad \text{(mA)}
    \label{eq:tx_current}
\end{equation}

The total energy per transmission:
\begin{equation}
    E = V \cdot I_{tx} \cdot T_{\text{air}} + E_{\text{rx}} \quad \text{(mJ)}
    \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}

where $V = 3.3$\,V is the supply voltage and $E_{\text{rx}} = 0.033$\,mJ is the receive window energy overhead.

This energy calculation is important because it is the same for all ADR methods -- the energy depend only on the chosen SF and TP, not on which algorithm chose them. Higher SF naturally result in longer airtime and therefore more energy consumption.

\subsubsection{Double Deep Q-Network with Prioritized Experience Replay (DDQN-PER)}
\label{sec:ddqn}
% ============================================================================

\subsubsubsection{Why Deep Q-Learning?}

The standard Q-learning algorithm maintain a table of Q-values for every state-action pair. However, in our problem, the state space is continuous (12-dimensional real-valued vector), which make tabular Q-learning impractical. Deep Q-Learning solve this by using a neural network $Q(s, a; \theta)$ to approximate the Q-value function.

The basic DQN update rule is:
\begin{equation}
    Q(s_t, a_t; \theta) \gets Q(s_t, a_t; \theta) + \alpha \left[ r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a'; \theta^{-}) - Q(s_t, a_t; \theta) \right]
    \label{eq:dqn_update}
\end{equation}

where $\theta$ are the main network parameters and $\theta^{-}$ are the target network parameters.

\subsubsubsection{Double DQN}

Standard DQN suffer from overestimation of Q-values because the same network is used for both selecting and evaluating the best action. Double DQN (DDQN) address this by using two networks:

\begin{equation}
    y_t^{\text{DDQN}} = r_t + \gamma Q\left(s_{t+1}, \underset{a'}{\arg\max}\; Q(s_{t+1}, a'; \theta), \theta^{-}\right)
    \label{eq:ddqn_target}
\end{equation}

The main network $\theta$ select the best action, but the target network $\theta^{-}$ evaluate that action. This reduce the overestimation bias significantly.

\subsubsubsection{Prioritized Experience Replay}

Standard experience replay sample transitions uniformly from the replay buffer. However, some transitions are more informative than others. Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) sample transitions with probability proportional to their temporal difference (TD) error:

\begin{equation}
    P(i) = \frac{p_i^{\alpha}}{\sum_k p_k^{\alpha}}
    \label{eq:per_prob}
\end{equation}

where $p_i = |\delta_i| + \epsilon$ is the priority of transition $i$, $\delta_i$ is the TD error, $\alpha$ control how much prioritization is used ($\alpha = 0.6$ in our implementation), and $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ ensure non-zero probability.

To compensate for the bias introduced by non-uniform sampling, importance sampling weights are used:

\begin{equation}
    w_i = \left(\frac{1}{N \cdot P(i)}\right)^{\beta}
    \label{eq:is_weights}
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ is annealed from 0.4 to 1.0 during training.

\subsubsubsection{Network Architecture}

The Q-network use a simple fully connected architecture:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Input layer: 6 features (simplified state for basic DDQN)
    \item Hidden layer 1: 128 neurons with ReLU activation
    \item Hidden layer 2: 64 neurons with ReLU activation
    \item Output layer: 15 neurons (one per discrete action)
\end{itemize}

For the enhanced version, we use a Dueling DQN architecture with 12 input features and 48 output actions.

\subsubsubsection{Dueling Network Architecture}

The Dueling DQN separate the Q-value into a state value $V(s)$ and an advantage $A(s, a)$:

\begin{equation}
    Q(s, a; \theta, \alpha, \beta) = V(s; \theta, \beta) + \left(A(s, a; \theta, \alpha) - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a'} A(s, a'; \theta, \alpha)\right)
    \label{eq:dueling}
\end{equation}

This architecture help the network learn which states are valuable without needing to learn the effect of every action in every state.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{DDQN-PER ADR Agent}
\label{alg:ddqn_per_adr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Learning rate $\alpha = 0.001$, discount $\gamma = 0.95$, $\epsilon$-decay $= 0.995$
\State Initialize main network $Q(\cdot; \theta)$ and target network $Q(\cdot; \theta^-)$
\State Initialize PER buffer $\mathcal{D}$ with capacity 10000
\For{each packet transmission of device $i$}
    \State Observe state $s_t$ from device history
    \State Select action $a_t = \begin{cases} \text{random} & \text{with probability } \epsilon \\ \arg\max_a Q(s_t, a; \theta) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
    \State Decode action to get new SF and TP
    \State Apply SF ceiling based on SNR: $\text{SF} \gets \min(\text{SF}_{\text{proposed}}, \text{SF}_{\text{ceiling}})$
    \State Apply parameters to device MAC/PHY layer
    \State Observe reward $r_t$ and next state $s_{t+1}$
    \State Store $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in $\mathcal{D}$ with priority $p = |\delta| + \epsilon$
    \State Sample minibatch of 32 transitions from $\mathcal{D}$ using prioritized sampling
    \State Compute DDQN targets: $y_j = r_j + \gamma Q(s_{j+1}, \arg\max_{a'} Q(s_{j+1}, a'; \theta); \theta^-)$
    \State Update $\theta$ using weighted SGD with importance sampling weights
    \State Update priorities in $\mathcal{D}$
    \If{update counter $\bmod$ 100 $= 0$}
        \State $\theta^- \gets \theta$ \Comment{Sync target network}
    \EndIf
    \State $\epsilon \gets \max(\epsilon_{\min}, \epsilon \cdot \epsilon_{\text{decay}})$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsubsection{How DDQN-PER Improve PDR}

The DDQN-PER agent learn to adjust SF and TP based on the channel conditions. When it detect high interference (through pre-TX RSSI and SNR measurements), it learn to increase SF for better sensitivity or increase TP for stronger signal. When conditions are good, it learn to reduce SF for less airtime and reduce TP for energy saving.

The key advantage over classical ADR is that the DDQN agent consider more features in its decision (RSSI variance, PDR trends, consecutive losses) and can learn complex relationships between these features and the optimal action. It also use the target network and PER to learn more stably and efficiently.

However, while DDQN-PER effectively improve PDR, it tend to use higher SF and TP combinations, which result in higher energy consumption. This is because the reward function primarily optimize for successful delivery.

\subsubsection{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)}
\label{sec:ppo}
% ============================================================================

\subsubsubsection{From Value-Based to Policy-Based}

DDQN-PER is a value-based method -- it learn the Q-value function and derive the policy from it. PPO is a policy gradient method that directly optimize the policy. This have several advantages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item It can handle continuous action spaces naturally
    \item It provide more stable training through clipped objective
    \item It use an actor-critic architecture for lower variance
\end{itemize}

\subsubsubsection{PPO Objective}

The PPO algorithm optimize the clipped surrogate objective:

\begin{equation}
    L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[\min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right]
    \label{eq:ppo_clip}
\end{equation}

where $r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}$ is the probability ratio between new and old policies, $\hat{A}_t$ is the estimated advantage, and $\epsilon = 0.2$ is the clipping parameter.

\subsubsubsection{Policy Network}

The policy network (actor) output the mean and log standard deviation of a Gaussian distribution for two continuous actions:

\begin{align}
    \text{SF}_{\text{continuous}} &= 9.5 + 2.5 \tanh(\mu_1 + e^{\sigma_1} \cdot z_1) & z_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \\
    \text{TP}_{\text{continuous}} &= 8.0 + 6.0 \tanh(\mu_2 + e^{\sigma_2} \cdot z_2) & z_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)
\end{align}

where $(\mu_1, \mu_2)$ and $(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)$ are output by the policy network.

The SF is then discretized by rounding: $\text{SF} = \text{round}(\text{clip}(\text{SF}_{\text{continuous}}, 7, 12))$.

\subsubsubsection{Value Network (Critic)}

The value network estimate the state value $V(s_t)$, which is used to compute the Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE):

\begin{equation}
    \hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}
    \label{eq:gae}
\end{equation}

where $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$ is the TD residual, $\gamma = 0.95$ is the discount factor, and $\lambda = 0.95$ is the GAE parameter.

\subsubsubsection{Log Probability Calculation}

The log probability of an action under the Gaussian policy is:

\begin{equation}
    \log \pi(a|s) = -\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{a - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^2 - \log\sigma - \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)
    \label{eq:log_prob}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{PPO ADR Agent}
\label{alg:ppo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Clip $\epsilon = 0.2$, learning rates: actor = 0.0003, critic = 0.001
\State Initialize actor $\pi_\theta$ and critic $V_\phi$
\For{each packet transmission of device $i$}
    \State Build state $s_t$ from device history (12 features)
    \State Sample $({\text{SF}}, {\text{TP}}) \sim \pi_\theta(s_t)$ using Gaussian sampling
    \State Apply SF ceiling: $\text{SF} \gets \min(\text{SF}_{\text{sampled}}, \text{SF}_{\text{ceiling}})$
    \State Calculate log probability $\log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)$
    \State Apply parameters and observe reward $r_t$
    \State Store transition $(s_t, a_t, \log\pi, r_t, V(s_t))$
    \If{trajectory buffer size $\geq 32$}
        \State Compute GAE advantages $\hat{A}_t$ using Eq.~\ref{eq:gae}
        \State Compute returns $G_t = \hat{A}_t + V(s_t)$
        \State Normalize advantages
        \State Update actor using clipped objective (Eq.~\ref{eq:ppo_clip})
        \State Update critic to minimize $(V_\phi(s_t) - G_t)^2$
        \State Clear trajectory buffer
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsubsection{PPO and Energy Efficiency}

PPO have an advantage over DDQN-PER in terms of energy efficiency because the continuous action space allow it to find intermediate TX power values instead of being limited to discrete choices. The policy can learn to output lower power values when the link quality permit. Combined with the congestion-adaptive reward function, PPO can better balance PDR and energy consumption.

\subsubsection{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)}
\label{sec:marl}
% ============================================================================

\subsubsubsection{From Single-Agent to Multi-Agent}

In the DDQN-PER and PPO approaches, each device have its own independent agent that make decisions without knowing what other devices are doing. This is fine when the interactions between devices are weak, but in dense deployment, the devices' actions strongly affect each other. When Device A change to SF7 on channel 0, it directly affect the collision probability for Device B if B is also on SF7 channel 0.

This motivate the multi-agent approach where each device still have its own agent, but the agents share coordination signals. This is known as Independent Q-Learning with coordination, and it turn the problem from an MDP into a Markov Game.

\subsubsubsection{Markov Game Formulation}

A Markov Game for $N$ agents is defined by $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{A}_N, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_N, \gamma)$ where each agent $i$ have its own action space $\mathcal{A}_i$ and reward function $\mathcal{R}_i$, but the state transition depend on the joint action of all agents.

In our case, each agent observe:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Its own local state (RSSI, SNR, PDR, current parameters) -- same 11 features as DDQN
    \item A coordination feature: the number of other devices using the same channel (normalized collision risk)
\end{enumerate}

The coordination feature is what make MARL different. It give each agent awareness of the network level situation without sharing the full state of every device (which would be impractical in a real system).

\subsubsubsection{MARL Agent Architecture}

Each MARL agent use the same Dueling DQN architecture as the enhanced DDQN agent, with 12 input features and 48 output actions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shared feature layers}: Two hidden layers that extract features from the input
    \item \textbf{Value stream}: Estimate the state value $V(s)$ with Leaky ReLU activation
    \item \textbf{Advantage stream}: Estimate the advantage $A(s, a)$ for each action
    \item \textbf{Output}: $Q(s, a) = V(s) + A(s, a) - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a'} A(s, a')$
\end{itemize}

\subsubsubsection{Coordination-Aware Action Selection}

The MARL agent modify the $\epsilon$-greedy exploration to be coordination-aware:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{MARL Coordination-Aware Action Selection}
\label{alg:marl_action}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Current channel $c$, channel usage counts for all devices
\State Compute channel congestion: $\text{count}[c] \gets |\{j \neq i : c_j = c\}|$
\If{$\text{count}[c] \geq 2$ \textbf{and} random() $< 0.4$}
    \State Find least congested channel: $c^* = \arg\min_k \text{count}[k]$
    \State \Return channel-change action for $c^*$ \Comment{Active collision avoidance}
\EndIf
\If{random() $< \epsilon$}
    \State \Return random action \Comment{Standard exploration}
\Else
    \State \Return $\arg\max_a Q(s_t, a; \theta)$ \Comment{Exploitation}
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This coordination mechanism mean that when a channel become congested (multiple devices on the same channel), the agents actively try to redistribute to less congested channels. This reduce collisions without requiring a central coordinator.

\subsubsubsection{Extended State Vector with Coordination}

The MARL agent state vector extend the standard state with coordination signals:

\begin{equation}
    s_t^{\text{MARL}} = \left[s_t^{\text{local}},\; \frac{n_{\text{same\_channel}}}{3}\right]
    \label{eq:marl_state}
\end{equation}

where $s_t^{\text{local}}$ is the 11-dimensional local state (same as PPO/DDQN) and $n_{\text{same\_channel}}$ is the count of other devices using the same channel, normalized by 3.

Additionally, the MARL agent compute the average PDR of other agents as contextual information:

\begin{equation}
    \overline{\text{PDR}}_{\text{others}} = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{j \neq i} \text{PDR}_j^{\text{recent}}
    \label{eq:avg_other_pdr}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{MARL ADR Agent -- Full Training Loop}
\label{alg:marl_full}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Learning rate $\alpha = 0.0005$, $\epsilon_0 = 0.3$, $\epsilon_{\text{decay}} = 0.999$, $\gamma = 0.95$
\State Initialize Dueling DQN main and target networks for each agent $i$
\State Initialize PER buffer $\mathcal{D}_i$ with capacity 20000 for each agent
\State Initialize channel tracking map for all devices
\For{each packet transmission of device $i$}
    \State Build coordinated state $s_t^{\text{MARL}}$ including collision risk feature
    \State Select action using coordination-aware policy (Algorithm~\ref{alg:marl_action})
    \State Decode action $\to$ (SF, TP, channel change)
    \State Apply SF ceiling based on SNR
    \State Update channel tracking: $\text{deviceChannels}[i] \gets c_{\text{new}}$
    \State Broadcast new channel to other agents
    \State Apply parameters and observe reward $r_t$
    \State Store $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in $\mathcal{D}_i$
    \State Sample minibatch and train using DDQN update rule
    \State Periodically sync target network ($\theta^- \gets \theta$ every 50 updates)
    \State $\epsilon \gets \max(0.05, \epsilon \cdot 0.999)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsubsection{Why MARL is Better for Energy Efficiency}

MARL achieve better energy efficiency than DDQN-PER and PPO for several reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Active collision avoidance through channel coordination}: By redistributing devices across channels, MARL reduce the need for higher SF (which increase airtime and energy) to overcome collisions.
    \item \textbf{Lower SF usage}: When devices are spread across different channels, each channel have less traffic, so devices can use lower SF values while maintaining good PDR.
    \item \textbf{Lower TX power}: With less co channel interference, devices do not need to boost their power as much to maintain a good SNR at the gateway.
    \item \textbf{Network level optimization}: Unlike single agent methods that optimize each device independently (potentially conflicting with each other), MARL agents collectively converge to a configuration that benefit the whole network.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Experiment 1: Dense Deployment with WiFi Interference}

This is the ultimate game scenario: 20 devices packed into just 100\,m radius, transmitting every 30 seconds, for a long simulation of 100,000 seconds. This is a highly competitive environment where every device is very close to every other device and to the gateway.

The results are striking:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classical ADR} achieve only 42.07\% with extremely high variance (46.12\%). This show that the classical algorithm is not designed for such dense scenarios.
    \item \textbf{All three RL methods achieve near-perfect delivery}: DDQN-PER (99.24\%), PPO (99.33\%), and MARL (99.63\%) all massively outperform classical ADR.
    \item \textbf{MARL achieve the highest PDR} at 99.63\% with the lowest variance (0.80\%), meaning every single device consistently deliver its packets.
    \item \textbf{DDQN-PER achieves high PDR but at enormous energy cost} (487,277\,mJ), approximately 6$\times$ more than MARL, because it aggressively increases SF and TP without coordination.
    \item \textbf{PPO and MARL achieve comparable energy to the baselines} (77,443 and 79,484\,mJ vs 79,500 for No ADR). This is remarkable -- they deliver nearly all packets while using the same energy as methods that deliver only 32--42\% of packets.
\end{itemize}

The reason MARL and PPO achieve this is the channel coordination and efficient parameter selection. In a 100\,m radius, all devices have good link quality (close to gateway), so the main challenge is collisions. MARL agents learn to spread across different channels and use low SF values (SF7), which minimize airtime and therefore both energy and collision probability. The agents effectively solve the frequency allocation problem through distributed learning. PPO similarly learns to use SF7 with appropriate TP, achieving excellent energy efficiency. DDQN-PER, lacking coordination, resorts to brute-force SF and TP increases that consume significantly more energy.

All RL methods show rapid initial improvement, with MARL converging faster due to its coordination signals. The rapid convergence is due to the pre-trained models: the agents leverage their training from previous simulations and only need to adapt their policies to the current network configuration.

\subsection{Physical Testbed Implementation}
\label{subsec:testbed}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{3 End Devices}: ESP32 microcontrollers connected to SX1278 LoRa transceiver modules. Each ESP32 run the RL-based ADR agent locally and can adjust its SF and TP based on the learned policy.
    \item \textbf{1 Gateway}: STM32 based Arduino Portenta H7, which receive the LoRa packets and forward the data to the network server over WiFi.
    \item \textbf{Network Server}: Running on a PC, connected to the gateway via WiFi. The network server process the received packets, calculate the metrics, and send ADR commands back to the devices.
\end{itemize}

The system is currently successfully updating the parameters in the end devices based on the RL policy. The ESP32 devices receive the ADR commands through the downlink and adjust their spreading factor and transmission power accordingly.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================================

In this research, we show that reinforcement learning is a powerful tool for optimizing the Adaptive Data Rate mechanism in LoRaWAN networks under dense deployment conditions. The key contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We identify the ADR parameter optimization in dense LoRaWAN as a Markov Decision Process (for single agent) and a Markov Game (for multi agent), and show that the game structure naturally arise when many devices compete for limited radio resources.
    
    \item We implement and compare three RL algorithms (DDQN-PER, PPO, MARL) against classical ADR on a realistic ns-3 simulation platform with WiFi and LTE mobile interference, across 11 diverse scenarios spanning 10 to 200 devices.
    
    \item We demonstrate that MARL with coordination signals achieve the best overall performance, with up to 99.63\% PDR in the most competitive scenario while maintaining energy consumption comparable to the no-ADR baseline. In 10 out of 11 test scenarios, MARL is the top-performing method.
    
    \item We conduct extensive scalability analysis showing that MARL degrades more gracefully than all alternatives as device count increases from 20 to 200. Even at 200 devices, MARL delivers 17.64\% of packets where classical ADR delivers virtually none (0.025\%). MARL also achieves the highest Jain's fairness index (0.547--0.662), confirming equitable resource distribution through coordination.
    
    \item We analyze convergence behavior and demonstrate that pre-trained RL agents converge within $\sim$16 packets per device, making the approach feasible for practical deployment without long warm-up periods.
    
    \item We validate the approach with a physical testbed using ESP32 devices and SX1278 LoRa modules, showing that the RL policy can be successfully deployed on real IoT hardware.
\end{enumerate}

The results clearly show that as IoT networks become denser and more dynamic (as expected in Industry 4.0), classical approaches will not be sufficient. Reinforcement learning, especially multi-agent approaches with coordination, provide a practical and effective solution for this problem. The scalability analysis further demonstrates that while absolute PDR degrades at extreme densities, the relative advantage of MARL over classical approaches actually increases with scale.
